### Q28. [3 Marks]
**Question:** Describe three key features of India's foreign trade on the eve of independence. Illustrate with examples the country's trading patterns with other nations.

**Answer:**
On the eve of independence, the structure, composition, and volume of India's foreign trade were governed by colonial British interests. The three key features were:

1. **Net Exporter of Primary Products and Importer of Finished Goods:**
   - India was reduced to being a feeder economy to British industries.
   - **Exports:** Raw agricultural and mineral products such as raw cotton, raw silk, raw jute, wool, sugar, indigo, and tea.
   - **Imports:** Finished manufactured consumer goods like cotton textiles, silk and woollen clothes from British mills, and capital goods manufactured in Britain.

2. **Monopoly Control of Britain over Foreign Trade:**
   - The colonial government maintained discriminatory tariff policies and restrictive monopoly control.
   - More than **half of India's foreign trade was restricted to Great Britain**.
   - The remaining limited trade was allowed with only a few countries such as **China, Ceylon (Sri Lanka), and Persia (Iran)**. The opening of the **Suez Canal in 1869** further intensified British dominance and lowered shipping costs between Britain and India.

3. **Generation of an Export Surplus Exploited for the "Drain of Wealth":**
   - India generated a large export surplus throughout the colonial rule, but this surplus did not benefit the Indian economy (no inflow of gold or silver).
   - The surplus was siphoned off by the British administration to cover administrative costs of the colonial government in London, expenses incurred on wars fought by the British army, and payments for invisible service imports, resulting in the **"Drain of Indian Wealth"**.

### Q29. [3 Marks]
**Question:** (b) *"While human capital is a means to an end, human development is an end in itself."* Elaborate the above statement, highlighting the difference between the two concepts with the help of suitable examples.

**Answer:**
The statement accurately highlights the fundamental philosophical and economic distinction between **Human Capital** and **Human Development**:

| Basis | Human Capital (Means to an end) | Human Development (End in itself) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Concept** | Treats human beings as instruments or productive inputs to accelerate output and economic growth. | Treats human beings as the ultimate objective of all development activities. |
| **Perspective on Education & Health** | Investment in education and health is justified only because it enhances labor productivity and increases worker output. | Education and basic healthcare are fundamental human rights, essential for well-being regardless of their direct contribution to GDP. |
| **Example** | Training an IT engineer or an industrial technician so they write software or produce factory goods faster to generate revenue. If an investment does not raise productivity, it is deemed ineffective under this view. | Providing education and medical care to a disabled person or an elderly citizen so they can lead a dignified, enlightened, and healthy life with choices, even if their labor productivity is zero. |

**Conclusion:** Therefore, human capital is merely a *means* to economic production, whereas human development is the true *end* goal of human life and society.

### Q30. [4 Marks]
**Question:** State four measures that a government in a developing country can implement to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of the agricultural marketing system.

**Answer:**
To eliminate malpractices by middlemen and protect farmers from distress sales, a government can implement the following four measures:

1. **Establishment of Regulated Markets:**
   - Establishing regulated markets where transparent and standardized weighing, fair trade practices, and auction procedures are enforced under market committees.
   - This eliminates exploitation by commission agents and fraudulent weighing.

2. **Development of Physical Infrastructure:**
   - Providing critical logistical infrastructure such as all-weather rural roads, modern warehousing facilities, cold storage chains, and food processing facilities.
   - This prevents post-harvest spoilage of perishables and allows farmers to store produce and sell when market prices are favorable.

3. **Promotion of Cooperative Agricultural Marketing:**
   - Encouraging cooperative marketing societies (e.g., successful models like AMUL milk cooperatives) where farmers pool their agricultural produce to bargain collectively.
   - Collective bargaining secures remunerative prices, lowers transportation costs, and provides access to grading and processing facilities.

4. **Policy Support Measures (Price Stabilisation & Direct Marketing):**
   - **Minimum Support Price (MSP) and Buffer Stocking:** Announcing MSP before the sowing season to set a price floor, with procurement agencies (like FCI) buying surplus crops.
   - **Alternative Direct Marketing Channels:** Promoting direct farm-to-consumer markets (e.g., *Apni Mandi* in Punjab/Haryana, *Rythu Bazars* in Andhra Pradesh, *Uzhavar Sandies* in Tamil Nadu) and national digital platforms like **e-NAM** (National Agriculture Market) to eliminate intermediary margins.

### Q31. [4 Marks]
#### Option (a):
**Question:** *"Before 1991, the Indian Banking sector was dominated by Public Sector Banks, regulated and controlled by the Reserve Bank of India."* Discuss the reforms undertaken by the Indian government in the year 1991 to improve the banking system.

**Answer:**
As part of the **Financial Sector Reforms** under the 1991 New Economic Policy (based on the Narasimham Committee recommendations), significant reforms were introduced to modernize the Indian banking system:

1. **Transformation in the Role of the RBI (Regulator to Facilitator):**
   - The role of the Reserve Bank of India shifted from an authoritative **regulator** to a market-friendly **facilitator**. 
   - Commercial banks were granted greater functional autonomy to take operational decisions without seeking prior approval for every routine activity.

2. **Entry of Private Sector Banks (Domestic and Foreign):**
   - The monopoly of public sector banks was ended by allowing the establishment of new private sector domestic banks (such as HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank) and international foreign banks. This introduced competitive efficiency and improved customer service standards.

3. **Liberalisation of Foreign Direct Investment (FDI):**
   - Foreign investment limits in the banking sector were liberalized (subsequently raised up to 74%), allowing foreign institutional investors and FDI into Indian commercial banking.

4. **Freedom in Branch Expansion and Rationalisation of Reserve Ratios:**
   - Banks that complied with prescribed capital adequacy and prudential norms were allowed to open new branches and rationalize existing networks without tedious prior permissions.
   - Statutory ratios such as **Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)** and **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** were substantially brought down from excessively high pre-1991 levels, leaving commercial banks with greater loanable funds for credit disbursement to productive sectors.

#### Option (b) [OR]:
**Question:** *"The enforcement of land ceiling laws in India faced numerous challenges which restricted their success."* Do you agree with the given statement? Give a valid explanation in support of your answer.

**Answer:**
**Yes, I fully agree with the given statement.** 

Land ceiling legislation was enacted to fix the maximum size of agricultural land an individual/family could own, with surplus land to be redistributed to landless cultivators. However, its implementation faced severe challenges and was largely restricted in its success due to:

1. **Protracted Legal Battles and Delays:**
   - Large landlords utilized their financial power to challenge the constitutionality of the land ceiling legislation in courts.
   - These protracted legal disputes gave big landholders ample time to transfer their surplus lands under the names of close relatives, friends, or fictitious persons (**benami transactions**), effectively evading the legal ceiling limits.

2. **Exploitation of Exemptions and Legal Loopholes:**
   - The legislation contained numerous exemption clauses for mechanized farms, plantations (tea, coffee, rubber), orchards, sugar mills, and religious/charitable trusts.
   - Landlords manipulated these legal loopholes to classify their prime cultivable agricultural lands under exempt categories to prevent acquisition by the government.

3. **Eviction of Tenants under "Self-Cultivation":**
   - Landlords utilized legislative clauses allowing resumption of land for "personal cultivation". They forcibly evicted small sharecroppers and tenants, rendering them landless agricultural laborers.

4. **Lack of Political Will and Administrative Collusion:**
   - In most Indian states, the local political leadership and revenue bureaucracy belonged to the dominant landowning elite. 
   - Consequently, there was poor administrative enforcement. The only notable exceptions were **Kerala and West Bengal**, where strong political will and grassroots peasant movements made land reforms genuinely successful.

    - In **urban areas**, **Regular Salaried Employees** form the greatest proportion (**47%**).
    *(Note: If evaluated as the single largest category of employment across the entire Indian workforce combined, **Self-employed** constitutes the highest overall share).*

- **(b) Discuss any two reasons behind this pattern. [3 Marks]**
  - **Answer:**
    1. **Prevalence of Agriculture and Family Landholdings in Rural Areas:**
       - Agriculture is the principal source of livelihood in rural India. Most rural households possess small family farms, ancestral land plots, or engage in village-level allied activities (dairy, poultry, handicraft). 
       - Since they cultivate their own land using family labor, they naturally fall under the category of **self-employed** (accounting for 58% of rural workers).
    2. **Concentration of Organized Industries, Services, and Skill Requirements in Urban Areas:**
       - Urban centers are hubs of manufacturing factories, corporate offices, public sector undertakings, educational institutions, and organized retail and financial services.
       - These organized enterprises require specific educational qualifications, technical capabilities, and administrative skills, and hire workers on long-term contracts with fixed monthly salaries and social benefits, making **regular salaried employment** the dominant category in urban areas (47%).
       - Conversely, rural areas lack formal, organized industrial establishments, making regular salaried employment scarce (only 13% in rural areas).

#### Option (b) [OR]:
- **(i) "In the 1950s, excessive government regulation prevented the growth of entrepreneurship." Do you agree with the given statement? Give a valid explanation in support of your answer. [3 Marks]**
  - **Answer:**
    - **Verdict:** **Yes, I agree with the statement.**
    - **Explanation:**
      1. **The Stifling "License-Permit-Quota Raj":** Under the Industrial Policy Resolution 1956 and the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act 1951, no new industrial enterprise could be established, expanded, or diversified without obtaining a license from the central bureaucracy.
      2. **Pre-emption of Licenses by Established Business Monopolies:** Influential established business houses abused the system by acquiring licenses not to produce goods, but to prevent new entrants and competitors from entering the market, effectively throttling dynamic young entrepreneurs.
      3. **Bureaucratic Red Tape and Misallocation of Time:** Rather than dedicating resources to research, quality enhancement, and technological innovation, prospective entrepreneurs had to spend excessive time, money, and effort lobbying multiple government ministries to obtain permits and raw material quotas.
      4. **Stringent Price and Production Controls:** Rigid price controls and strict production quotas undermined the profit motive and financial viability of innovative businesses, severely dampening the entrepreneurial spirit of post-independence India.

- **(ii) "The tax reductions in the reform period have not resulted in an increase in tax revenue." Justify the given statement with a valid explanation. [3 Marks]**
  - **Answer:**
    - **Justification / Explanation:** The post-1991 fiscal and tax reforms aimed at reducing individual income tax rates and corporate tax rates under the assumption that lower tax rates would incentivize voluntary compliance and curb tax evasion, thereby enlarging the aggregate tax net. However, this did not produce the expected proportional increase in tax revenues due to the following reasons:
      1. **Limited Rise in Tax Compliance:** Lowering direct tax rates did not result in a dramatic expansion of the tax base or a proportional surge in voluntary compliance. The overall tax-to-GDP ratio in India remained largely stagnant throughout the reform decade.
      2. **Sharp Slashing of Customs Tariffs:** In line with WTO commitments and trade liberalization policies, the Indian government dramatically lowered import tariffs, peak customs duties, and quantitative restrictions. This caused a substantial direct loss of revenue from customs duties, which had traditionally formed a large component of government indirect tax revenues.
      3. **Tax Exemptions and Incentives to Encourage Investment:** To attract foreign investment (FDI/FII) and stimulate private sector capital formation, the government granted extensive tax holidays, special economic zone (SEZ) exemptions, and corporate concessions, which curtailed potential gross tax collections.
